Yet they have treated it as a looming threat rather than one already eroding the very foundations on which Europe stands.Russia’s war has devastated much of Ukraine’s south and east  even as the front lines have shifted only slowly. For Europe  however  the challenge has never been confined to the battlefield. Supporting Ukraine and constraining Russia have tested the continent’s economic resources and political unity. Now  as United States President Donald Trump seeks to force a disadvantageous settlement on Kyiv and repeatedly threatens to withdraw US support  Europe faces the prospect of shouldering even more of that burden alone.Even if the latest crisis is averted  Europe can no longer assume that Washington will guarantee its security. It must develop the financial capacity to sustain Ukraine’s defence  the industrial strength to defend itself and the economic leverage to constrain Moscow. To meet these challenges  Europe must build a war economy.The past four years offer lessons in how Europe can finance such an effort  but also warnings about the divisions that could undermine it. The European Union has already been at the forefront of Ukraine’s recent financing  moving from an 18.1-billion-euro ($21bn) share of the G7’s $50bn Extraordinary Revenue Acceleration (ERA) loans in 2024 to finalising a new 90-billion-euro ($100bn) loan this April  one that came without US commitment. Yet internal divisions remain over numerous issues  particularly as they pertain to constraining Russia. This month  the EU adopted its largest-ever round of sanctions listings. But while the measures added a record number of designations  they did little to broaden the scope of restricted activity and did not accelerate the phase-out of Russian energy imports  with pipeline gas imports still permitted until autumn 2027. European leaders have also expressed concern over Trump’s decision to waive new Russia-related sanctions as he seeks to grapple with the costs his foreign policy has wrought.AdvertisementHowever  the US-Israel war on Iran has  if anything  shown that Europe’s measures against Russian hydrocarbons could have gone further. Before February  some 20 million barrels per day (bpd) of crude and oil products and roughly 110 billion cubic metres (bcm) a year of liquefied natural gas (LNG) passed through the Strait of Hormuz. By comparison  Russia exported about 6.5 million bpd of oil and products in September  and some 43bcm of LNG last year – about a third of Hormuz’s oil and two-fifths of its LNG. As the crisis continues and Trump seeks additional Russian supplies  Europe must complement its efforts to constrain Moscow with greater investment in its own security.The first and most important step is to establish a standing framework for joint EU borrowing – not only to finance Ukraine’s continued defence  but also to fund the defence industry Europe will need to sustain its own defence and  if necessary  prevail against future threats from Russia. Crisis begets innovation  as demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic  when EU member states pushed past their long-held aversion to mutualised borrowing  creating the 750-billion-euro NextGenerationEU recovery fund in 2020. The EU’s contribution to the ERA loans and this year’s 90-billion-euro facility  both financed by common EU borrowing  extended that logic to wartime finance. But these remain crisis-by-crisis improvisations. Deepening fiscal and capital markets union and building a genuine fiscal-military state would provide the foundations of Europe’s power and its ability to deter and win wars.The same logic plays out in the US today  where deep capital markets have given companies such as SpaceX  which invested heavily for years before seeing returns  the space to develop technologies such as its Starlink network that have reshaped modern warfare. The importance of this technology is now keenly felt on Ukraine’s battlefields  where Kyiv’s communications have rested on a network subject to the whims of one man  SpaceX CEO Elon Musk. Europe therefore needs the fiscal capacity to fund its own technological development and procurement.There are understandable jitters about the existing indebtedness of European states such as France and the United Kingdom. However  Russia’s frozen sovereign assets offer a potential way to support further borrowing. Divisions remain over using these assets  with Belgium  which hosts the lion’s share at Euroclear  opposing such a move over fears of Russian legal action and damage to its position in the global financial system. The bloc must come together to share that risk  which is overblown in any case. Not all EU fiscal support needs to come in the form of direct joint debt either. Greatly expanding the EU capital markets union and creating tax-advantaged structures across the region for defence firms and technological innovators may prove even more effective.AdvertisementBeyond the assets themselves  Europe must also take greater control over its continued economic linkages with Russia. That means intervening far more actively in cases such as that of Austria’s Raiffeisen Bank. In 2025  following a Russian court ruling  more than 2.1 billion euros were taken from Raiffeisen’s Russian subsidiary to compensate Rasperia  a company tied to the sanctioned oligarch Oleg Deripaska. A Vienna court’s ruling last month now paves the way for Raiffeisen to seek compensation from Rasperia’s frozen assets in the EU – using the same sanctions derogation through which Euroclear released some $3bn in 2025. It is precisely this kind of legalised dealing with Moscow that keeps threats like those levelled at Belgium credible: if the Kremlin can seize European assets and prompt the release of Russian-linked assets frozen in the EU  every expropriation becomes a bargaining chip.Furthermore  the UK and the EU have increasingly made use of measures against circumvention that share some features with US secondary sanctions. Europe should now be more assertive in making it clear that firms helping Russia evade its sanctions risk losing access to European financial systems and markets. US firms will choose Europe over the Kremlin  even if Trump waives more sanctions.Finally  Europe should seek to reduce US influence over its foreign and security relationships. Speaking in Qatar this week  US Ambassador to Turkiye Tom Barrack said Washington was moving away from its decades-old bargain of security for Gulf oil. Europe may not be able to offer the same level of security  but it can offer a mutually beneficial partnership that addresses both sides’ concerns. And it is Europe that is now stepping up in the Gulf  with the UK and France deploying jets  air defences and refuelling support to help Saudi Arabia against Houthi attacks. Unlike Washington  Europe can promise a deeper partnership in the future and serve as a reliable buyer of Gulf oil and gas exports in exchange for Gulf states doing more to address Europe’s security concerns about Russia through enhanced sanctions compliance and  first and foremost  ending the Kremlin’s role in OPEC plus.Europe has the means to finance its own security. It must now find the will to borrow for it.The views expressed in this article are the author’s own and do not necessarily reflect Al Jazeera’s editorial At the Musée d'Art Moderne de Paris  the Marcel Duchamp Prize ceremony stands as one of the week's major events  opening a discussion that will continue long after the fair.A Major Contemporary Art Prize Since 2000Established in 2000 by ADIAF  the Association pour la diffusion internationale de l'art français  and organized in partnership with the Centre Pompidou from the outset  the Marcel Duchamp Prize promotes the French art scene internationally. While the Centre Pompidou's historic Beaubourg building undergoes renovation  the Musée d'Art Moderne de Paris is hosting the exhibitions of shortlisted artists and the announcement of the winner from 2025 through 2029.With a total endowment of €90 000  including €35 000 awarded to the winner  the Prize recognizes four artists or artist collectives each year. ADIAF extends this support through international exhibitions and residencies  notably with Villa Albertine in the United States and the Manufacture de Sèvres. Past winners include Dominique Gonzalez-Foerster  Tatiana Trouvé  Clément Cogitore  Tarik Kiswanson  Gaëlle Choisne  and Xie Lei. Kader Attia  the 2016 winner  served on this year's jury  chaired by Xavier Rey  Director of the Musée national d'art moderne.Making the Time of Abstinence VisibleDavid Brognon  born in Belgium in 1978  and Stéphanie Rollin  born in Luxembourg in 1980  met at Mudam and have worked together since 2006. Represented by the gallery mfc-michèle didier  they have developed a practice attentive to waiting  confinement  and people living on the margins of society. Their works take shape through encounters with individual lives and circumstances.Their installation  titled 5 376 Years  1 892 Months  1 110 Days  1 201 Hours  adds together the periods inscribed on the sobriety tokens they have been collecting since 2022. Awarded by support groups to mark milestones in abstinence  these objects bear witness to fragile victories and efforts sustained day after day.In the dimly lit space  four carnival machines hold the tokens. Their mechanisms keep them in a precarious balance: they never fall. Three wall lights are connected to Kate  who has been sober for eight years and remotely adjusts their intensity according to what she is experiencing. The installation brings the rhythm of an actual human life into the exhibition.Each token thus represents a period of lived time. The possibility of falling  the waiting  and the perseverance become perceptible. The work opens a meditation on suffering  the effort to hold on  and the vulnerability of existence  extending to the question of mortality.A Conceptual Work in the Tradition of Marcel DuchampIn an intimate and sensitive conversation with thierry Ehrmann  Claude Bonnin  President of ADIAF  emphasized the connection between this work and Marcel Duchamp's approach  as well as its two intertwined dimensions: fully conceptual and profoundly human.The Marcel Duchamp Prize has always embraced a diversity of practices  from painting and sculpture to video and installation. With Brognon Rollin  the connection to the readymade is particularly clear: preexisting objects  imbued with uses and histories  are relocated and brought together in an arrangement that transforms their meaning. The thought that selects them and establishes relationships among them becomes constitutive of the work.Marcel Duchamp  a foundational figure for conceptual art  profoundly reshaped the definition of the artistic act. With the readymade  choosing an object  designating it as art  and presenting it in a different context can constitute an act of creation. The work's meaning then engages perception and thought as much as its material fabrication.Brognon Rollin brings a distinctive human depth to this shift. The token leaves its original use behind and carries the trace of an intimate struggle into public space. The rigor of the installation makes palpable what a number alone cannot convey: the weight of a day endured  the uncertainty of tomorrow  the determination to continue. Stéphanie Rollin sums up this approach in a single sentence: ""Our primary material is the human being.""From the Conceptual Act to the Pen Entrusted to AIFor Artprice  this edition of the Marcel Duchamp Prize extends the discussion into writing: how do we define the act of creation when human thought gives meaning to a form that has not been made entirely by the creator's own hand?This question runs through Dialogue Between a Thinker and AI  the world-book by thierry Ehrmann  founder of Artprice and creator of La Demeure du Chaos. Over forty days and forty nights of dialogue  nearly four hundred and fifty hours  he brought his memories  archives  reasoning  and contradictions into an exchange with artificial intelligence. In the introductory text The Entrusted Pen  he describes his decision to entrust the writing to AI while continuing the work of thinking  challenging  and correcting  and assuming responsibility for the content.The connection Artprice proposes concerns the role of intention  experience  and meaning in the creative act. In Brognon Rollin's work  the selection and arrangement of objects make the traces of human lives accessible. In the book  a spoken voice and personal archives sustain a dialogue whose writing is entrusted to AI. In each case  the reader or visitor is invited to look beyond the production process and consider what is being conveyed.A token can count the days of abstinence; it cannot  on its own  contain all the effort they required. Artificial intelligence can give written form to the story of a life; it has not lived the existence from which that story emerges. This gap gives the question of transmission its full significance: how can deeply personal experience be made shareable?Duchamp's legacy allows us to pose this question with renewed clarity. Creation can involve the selection  arrangement  and articulation of thought. In the age of AI  this reflection extends to the author's responsibility  the experiences the author brings  and the meaning for which the author answers.Through its support for the Marcel Duchamp Prize  Artprice backs artists who renew our ways of seeing. With Dialogue Between a Thinker and AI  thierry Ehrmann offers readers an experiment that questions our ways of writing and passing on experience. The encounter between these two approaches opens a shared space for reflection on the place of human beings in creation.An Edition Centered on Human ExperienceCurated by Odile Burluraux and Nicolas Liucci-Goutnikov  the exhibition also features Joël Andrianomearisoa  who explores the materiality of emotions through textiles  architecture  and writing; Laura Henno  whose photographs and films follow  among others  people living clandestinely in the Comoros and Mayotte; and Josèfa Ntjam  who combines archives  African mythologies  and science fiction to shift narratives of origin.Visit the Exhibition and Read the BookThe exhibition of the four artists shortlisted for the 2026 Marcel Duchamp Prize is on view at the Musée d'Art Moderne de Paris through February 7  2027  as part of its partnership with the Centre Pompidou and ADIAF.
