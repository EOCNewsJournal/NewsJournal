With governments  hospitals  stockbrokers  railway operators and other businesses scrambling for alternatives  the scenes that emerged from the collapse of Microsoft’s services were somewhat surreal.On one hand  employees were celebrating an unexpected day off. On the other  countries such as Japan  renowned for their technological sophistication  resorted to solutions that younger generations may not even know how to operate: landline telephones and fax machines  which  it is worth noting  were invented in 1864 and have been in use since the 19th century  or the handwritten  often illegible prescriptions that pharmacists somehow manage to decipher.This return to traditional methods was the only backup plan available as Microsoft was receiving 20 000 reports of problems every passing minute from every corner of the planet.Today  a day later  the vast majority of Microsoft’s services have been restored. In the early hours of the morning  the company reported that it was seeing a “steady recovery” across all affected environments. And although this may be a useful lesson for those who have never experienced life without technology making everything quick and easy  the question is whether we have ultimately invested so heavily in technology that our societies have become entirely dependent on it.Before answering that question with what might seem obvious  it is worth examining what actually happened. The problem began on the afternoon of Thursday  12 November  in the evening Greek time  initially in the United States. Within approximately an hour  reports of problems or complete service failures were pouring into Microsoft from 74 countries. More than 7.4 million reports were recorded in total  peaking at almost 20 000 per minute.The failure affected Exchange Online  Microsoft’s cloud-based email and calendar service  and from there disrupted or delayed Outlook  Teams  Copilot and a range of connected applications. Organisations hosting Microsoft services on their own servers were largely spared.What was most striking  however  was that the world’s most digitally advanced countries did not always have a digital backup plan.
