Is it enough?,Having fallen behind in the AI revolution  quantum could offer the continent a second chance at supremacy in an emerging technology.,In September 2017  Google researcher Austin Fowler stood before a room of quantum engineers at a conference in College Park  Md.  and admitted something rarely heard from Big Tech: He was stuck. Quantum computers make mistakes constantly  and every error must be caught within a millionth of a second before the whole calculation collapses. The best chip available was still 10 times too slow  Fowler said. Many in the room left convinced the problem might never be solved.But across the Atlantic  on a quiet street in Cambridge  a five-person startup was developing its own solution to the problem. Steve Brierley  a Cambridge University research fellow  had founded Riverlane from his kitchen table the year before. “Even Google can’t solve this problem ” he recalls thinking when he heard Fowler’s admission. In 2024  Riverlane ran what it describes as the world’s first low-latency quantum error-correction experiment  decoding errors in under a microsecond— fast enough to keep the machine running.Today  Riverlane employs nearly 200 people and works with more than 60% of the world’s quantum computer makers.With U.S. tech companies dominating the digital economy  and China and America pulling ahead in the race for AI supremacy  quantum computing represents a rare opportunity for Europe to establish itself at the forefront of a transformative technology. The European Commission’s Quantum Europe Strategy  aims to make the continent a global quantum leader by 2030  and McKinsey estimates the technology could create $2.7 trillion of economic value worldwide by 2035.The most advanced quantum computers hang like gilded chandeliers  their cores chilled to a fraction of a degree above absolute zero  more than 100 times as cold as deep space. Quantum technology builds on the principles of quantum mechanics and the ability of particles to act as both a particle and wave. Quantum computers harness this capability with qubits  allowing them to tackle problems that are prohibitively difficult for conventional machines  such as simulating molecules to accelerate drug discovery or modeling complex financial risks.Development of a quantum computer that is sufficiently powerful and reliable to complete these tasks would have strategic implications for nations  as it would theoretically be capable of breaking the encryption protecting banks and digital communications.“The company that builds the first large-scale quantum machine could seize an entire industry overnight ” says Jérémie Guillaud  an architect at Alice & Bob  one of France’s most promising quantum startups. Guillaud believes that the winner could be European. “There is a good possibility.”“We haven’t lost the race yet ” says Lionel ­ Martellini  a finance professor with a PhD in astrophysics who founded the EDHEC Quantum Institute in France. “Which doesn’t mean that we’re not going to lose it.”Quantum was “born in Europe ” Martellini notes  and the continent still produces much of the field’s foundational research. Quantum theory grew out of the work of Max Planck  Niels Bohr  Albert Einstein  Erwin Schrödinger  and Werner Heisenberg in the early 20th century. European physicists have since collected a string of quantum Nobel prizes.$2.7 T The value QUANTUM COMPUTING COULD DELIVER TO COMPANIES WORLDWIDE BY 203532% PERCENTAGE OF WORLD’S QUantum COmpanies Based in the EUSource: McKinsey: The Joint Research CentreThat legacy has seeded a thriving startup scene. The EU is home to 32% of the world’s quantum technology companies  according to the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre.France alone has five leading hardware contenders—Pasqal  Alice & Bob  Quandela  Quobly  and C12—each developing a different way to build a qubit  the basic unit of a quantum computer. Britain has Oxford Quantum Circuits and Riverlane  and Finland’s IQM says it has sold 26 quantum computers for customers to install on-site  the most publicly disclosed by any manufacturer.“Europe has a tremendous opportunity with quantum ” says Ana Paula Assis  senior vice president and chair for IBM Europe  Middle East  Africa  and Asia Pacific. “The region’s diverse industrial base holds both the prob- lems quantum computers are built to solve and the data needed to solve them ” she says.That has made Europe the industry’s biggest customer base. Two-fifths (43%) of quantum computing companies’ customers are headquartered on the continent  according to McKinsey’s Quantum Technology Monitor 2026. Much of that demand comes from public supercomputing centers  which allow researchers and companies to rent machines from European makers such as IQM and Pasqal.Illustration by Nicolas RappEuropean nations have committed $15.9 billion to quantum technologies in the five years to 2025. U.S. federal funding had reached only about $6 billion until May  when Washington pledged a further $2 billion to nine quantum companies.However  private investment in quantum startups is still dominated by the U.S. A record $12.6 billion was invested worldwide in 2025  more than six times the 2024 total  with 64% going to American companies  according to McKinsey. The European Commission estimates that European quantum firms attract only about 5% of global private funding.“American startups can draw on pension funds and university endowments  but there is no real European equivalent. We don’t have a unified marketplace  and we don’t have a very strong pension system ” Martellini says.“In France we have ideas  but no money ” says Alice & Bob’s Guillaud. “We won’t build a useful quantum computer just using ideas.” Alice & Bob’s €100 million ($110 million) Series B  one of Europe’s larger quantum rounds  is dwarfed by the $1 billion the California company PsiQuantum raised in a single round in September 2025.Consequently  many of Europe’s brightest prospects have crossed the Atlantic for capital. Britain’s Oxford Ionics was sold to U.S.-listed IonQ for $1.075 billion in 2025  and this year Finland’s IQM and France’s Pasqal both floated on Nasdaq. IQM also trades in Helsinki  and Pasqal is planning a second listing in Paris.Wasiq Bokhari  Pasqal’s chief executive  insists it was never a choice between New York and Paris. “Nasdaq just happened to be our first public listing on the way to our eventual Euronext listing ” he says. “Once a quantum company reaches the commercial stage it’s important to get access to capital at scale.”The bigger problem  according to Bokhari  is Europe’s scattered funding model  which is split across national plans.“The U.S. and China are single entities able to make decisions quickly ” Bokhari says. “Europe has all the ingredients. We just need to make sure we align them quickly and in a unified direction.” The EU’s Quantum Act  which aims to coordinate research and spending across the bloc  could help.The frontier  meanwhile  is racing ahead. The most powerful quantum computers remain in the U.S. and China  led by IBM  Google  Quantinuum  and the Chinese Academy of Sciences. IBM is building its first large- scale  error-corrected machine in Poughkeepsie  N.Y.  which will be capable of running 20 000 times as many operations as today’s machines.Wasiq Bokhari  CEO of Pasqal COURTESY OF PASQALAlessandro Curioni  vice president of IBM Research Europe and Africa  says this puts the company “two to five years ahead” of the pack on top-class hardware. That  he argues  means Europe risks “running the wrong race.” In his view  “the real prize is not the machines but a new software industry combining AI and quantum computing.” Europe’s diverse industrial economy gives it the best shot at leading that industry  he says  “but only if it starts now  on whatever machines are best.”“Europe has all the ingredients. We just need to make sure we align them quickly and in a unified direction.” Wasiq bokhari  CEO  PAsqalHe calls Europe’s instinct to build everything itself “arrogant ” and says refusing a machine because it comes from the U.S. would be “politically stupid.” If Europe waits for its own hardware  he warns  “you will have another Microsoft that is not going to be in Europe  that is going to sell you the software.”However  Pasqal’s Bokhari believes the race is far from over. “Heavy spending by big companies does not necessarily trans- late to a seat at the table or market dominance down the road.” European firms are still winning commercial customers  he adds.Crédit Agricole’s investment bank has signed a deal with Pasqal that aims to put its first quantum applications into live production as early as 2028. Bokhari says this makes it one of the first major banks to commit publicly to a production timeline. “Not only is it possible  but it is happening.”Quantum technology is also a potential threat to national security. Quantum sensors could eventually allow submarines  missiles  and military aircraft to navigate with greater precision than GPS  and quantum’s ability to break encryption could enable bad actors to intercept state messages.That makes ownership a question of sovereignty  says Martellini. “I don’t see why the world should be happy to rely on U.S.- based hardware. Europe has learned the hard way it needs to be more independent.”Europe’s greatest edge may be in supplying the critical components required to build quantum computers. Brierley compares it to the model of ASML  the Dutch company whose extreme ultraviolet lithography machines are essential for making the world’s most advanced AI chips.“Every new industry starts with companies building everything themselves  because there is no supply chain ” he says. “As the technology grows more complex  it splits into specialist layers  and whoever owns an indispensable layer gains enormous leverage.”Riverlane’s error-correction technology  for example  works across different types of qubit. Elsewhere  Finland’s Bluefors produces the gilded chandeliers that cool the world’s quantum computers  while Switzerland’s Zurich Instruments and the Netherlands’ Qblox supply the advanced electronics needed to run quantum hardware.It is a potentially much stronger position than Europe occupies in AI. EU companies supply nearly half the hardware and software components used in quantum technology worldwide  according to the European Commission. By contrast  EU firms account for less than a tenth of the region’s market for data center chips  servers  and cloud infrastructure  according to the Global Electronics Association. Owning a critical piece of the quantum supply chain  Brierley says  would give Europe “table stakes in a globally important technology.”An ordinary computer bit is like a coin lying flat: It is either heads or tails  0 or 1. A qubit is more like a coin spinning in the air  capable of holding both possibilities at once until it lands on one. The quantum industry is in a similar state of uncertainty.
